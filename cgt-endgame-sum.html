ENDGAME SUM

When a pawn ending splits into separate battles, add up their values

Some king-and-pawn endings fall apart into zones the kings can't cross in time to affect each other. Each zone then has a combinatorial game value — a number, or an infinitesimal like ↑ or * — and the values simply add. The sign of the sum, not the search tree, tells you who's winning.

Positive values favour White; negative favour Black. This is exactly the sign convention Elkies uses for Left/White in his endgame analysis.

Where this comes from

The publication

Noam D. Elkies, "On Numbers and Endgames: Combinatorial Game Theory in Chess Endgames," in Games of No Chance (R. J. Nowakowski, ed.), MSRI Publications vol. 29, Cambridge University Press, 1996, pp. 135–150 — also on arXiv as math/9905198. Elkies takes John Conway's theory of games and surreal numbers, built for things like Go and Nim, and applies it to king-and-pawn endgames where the board splits into pieces the kings can't reach each other across in time.

Why the arithmetic here is valid

In combinatorial game theory, disjoint games add: the value of a whole position that splits into independent zones is the sum of the zones' values, and only the sign of that sum (not the internal detail of any one zone) decides who wins the sum overall. A nonzero settled number always outweighs any amount of ↑/↓/*, because those are infinitesimal by construction — smaller in absolute terms than any positive number, however small. That's why this tool checks the plain number total first, and only falls back to the infinitesimal total, and then to the star, when the level above it is exactly zero. This resolution order is the standard "atomic weight" argument for sums of ups and stars (Berlekamp, Conway & Guy, Winning Ways, and Elkies' paper adapts it directly to pawn races and corresponding-square battles).

What doesn't fit

Elkies is explicit that this only works where the position truly decomposes — most chess positions are far too "hot" and interconnected for their zones to be scored independently; a queenside pawn race and a kingside king hunt in a middlegame don't add like this. The tool assumes you've already identified genuinely independent zones, the way Elkies does in his worked pawn-endgame examples.
