How this maps onto interaction nets

The rule the exchange follows

At each point in a capture sequence, the side about to recapture has a choice: take, or leave it. The mover wants the biggest net gain; the defender wants the mover's gain as small as possible. Worked backward from the last possible capture, that's V(i) = max(CM(i), V(i+1)) when it's the mover's turn to decide, and V(i) = min(CM(i), V(i+1)) when it's the defender's — where CM(i) is the material banked if the exchange stops after capture i. This is exactly the kind of small, closed, two-input-one-output fold interaction nets are built to run: a chain of agents, each with a principal port and two auxiliary ports, annihilating on contact.

Why this fits and a full search doesn't

Lafont's interaction nets are agents connected by wires, rewritten by local rules that fire only when two agents' principal ports meet — and those rules are strongly confluent, so the reduction order never changes the result. A capture sequence on one square is a genuinely local, small, terminating computation: exactly that shape. Deciding on a best chess move in general isn't — it needs move generation, a large branching game tree, and a heuristic evaluation function, none of which reduce to a small fixed net. So this tool applies the theory to the one piece of chess calculation that actually matches its shape, rather than forcing it onto search.

Reading the diagram

Each numbered box is a capture event, holding the material banked if the exchange stopped there (CM). The diamond between the last two boxes is the active agent — MAX if the mover is deciding, MIN if the defender is. Pressing Step fires it: the two boxes annihilate and are replaced by one box holding the outcome, exactly as two interaction-net agents vanish and leave their result wire behind. Keep stepping until one box remains — that's the SEE score.
